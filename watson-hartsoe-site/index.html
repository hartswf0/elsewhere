The opening film, in words

A short hand-drawn cartoon you move through by scrolling. Its scenes, in order:

  1. A figure with square glasses and an orange scarf stands on an empty page. Title: Interfaces for worlds that don't exist yet, and the tools to find out if they should. Watson Hartsoe, digital media artist and researcher, PhD candidate in Digital Media at Georgia Tech.
  2. He looks at the empty page. How does one person describe a world strongly enough that another person can enter it?
  3. He crouches and draws a hole around his own feet. Description has acquired consequences.
  4. He falls through the lines of a poem: “a world, described strongly enough that someone else could walk in. Here is a field the colour of paper, a road that only goes forward, a cinema, a gumball machine, a door, trees heavy with everything I have made. Every noun a floor. Every verb a wall. Say it slowly and it starts to hold you. The page gives way, and you are inside the description now.” He lands in a landscape. The poem becomes a place.
  5. He walks. My medium is context.
  6. He stops at a cinema showing five films: Where You Go When You Leave, CINEOSIS, Play, Freedom, and AI Films, The Machinery of Meaning, and Machinery of Meaning: Fluid.
  7. He walks past four systems: the Gumball Emotion Machine, Can a Model Build with LEGO?, Centaur Box, and Coaxing the Ripples. I do not understand context engineering as giving the model more detail. I understand it as applying pressure against the model's default world.
  8. A signpost points to four pieces of writing: Operative Ekphrasis, After the Scene, Growing Entanglements, and Auditing Emotion AI. Authorship in these systems is distributed, but responsibility is not.
  9. He walks into a grove of orange trees. Everything I have made is still on the tree.
  10. At the grove's gate he meets someone and hands over his pencil. Not in the prompt. Not in the output. In the encounter that changes what can happen next. Walk into the grove.

Read thickly: field notes on the film

  • A figure, a blank page. He is not illustrating the poem; he is standing in it.
  • He draws a hole around his own feet. Thin: a doodle. Thick: an author writing himself out of the frame.
  • Falling through the poem’s lines. The description now has consequences for the one inside it.
  • Landing changes the genre: above, a page; below, a place.
  • A cinema of verbs. The portrait in the lobby is the fieldworker, inside the field.
  • Four machines, one question: what does a model do with a description it was given?
  • The signpost points both ways. The essays are directions, not destinations.
  • Everything still on the tree: kept, not curated away.
  • He hands over the pencil. Authorship distributed; responsibility not. The difference between a twitch and a wink is not in the eyelid (after Clifford Geertz).

About

Watson Hartsoe is a research engineer and creative technologist who builds working tools for generative media, filmmaking, and worldbuilding. He is a PhD candidate in Digital Media at Georgia Tech, where he develops systems for a problem now shared across creative and technical fields: machines can produce more images, video, code, and possible worlds than people can meaningfully inspect, organize, or shape. His recent work turns that abundance into usable instruments, including browser-based filmmaking systems, networked 3D environments, and compilers that translate formal languages into interactive experiences. He holds an MS in Human-Computer Interaction from Georgia Tech and previously conducted Fulbright fieldwork in Honduras. His work has been recognized by the ITU AI for Good platform, Georgia Tech's AI Competition, CREATE-X, and the Neu Wave AI Film Festival.

    Research

    My research studies how people work with generative systems: how a prompt becomes a description that participates in producing what it describes, and how that changes when the cost of generating images, video, and interactive worlds approaches zero. I develop this through a method I call thick prompting, a documented and repeatable way of studying how a model interprets, transforms, and resists human intention.

    I work by building. Rather than writing about a tool in the abstract, I build the working system, test it with real users, observe where it fails, and revise it. This approach comes from earlier fieldwork: before human-computer interaction, I conducted Fulbright ethnographic research in Honduras, which taught me to treat any technology as part of the institutions, communities, and constraints it operates within, not as an isolated object.

    The through-line across the work is making opaque computational processes tangible, testable, and accountable, so that the assumptions inside a system become something people can see, question, and revise.
